**Anattā** ({{lang|Pāli|anattā}}), "non-self", is the third of the three characteristics ({{term|tilakkhaṇa}}), together with [[Anicca|impermanence]] and [[Dukkha|suffering]]. It is the teaching that nothing whatever is a self, belongs to a self, or contains a self. The Blessed One set it out systematically in the second discourse, the [[Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta]], at the end of which the five bhikkhus attained arahantship.[^sn2259]

:::quote Dhammapada, verse 279
*Sabbe dhammā anattā ti, yadā paññāya passati;*
*atha nibbindati dukkhe, esa maggo visuddhiyā.*

When one sees with wisdom that all phenomena are non-self, one becomes disenchanted with suffering. This is the path to purity.
:::

## The scope of non-self

The verses on impermanence and suffering speak of formations ({{term|saṅkhārā}}), but the verse on non-self speaks of all phenomena ({{term|dhammā}}). The difference is significant: formations are conditioned, while phenomena include the unconditioned. Nibbāna is not impermanent and not suffering, but it too is non-self. The Parivāra of the Vinaya states the conclusion:[^pariv]

:::quote Parivāra
*Aniccā sabbe saṅkhārā, dukkhānattā ca saṅkhatā;*
*nibbānañceva paññatti, anattā iti nicchayā.*

All formations are impermanent; the conditioned are suffering and non-self. Nibbāna and concepts too are non-self: this is certain.
:::

## The teaching in the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta

{{main|Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta}}

The Blessed One gave two lines of reasoning to the five bhikkhus at Isipatana:[^sn2259]

1. **Lack of control.** If form were self, it would not lead to affliction, and one could have it of form: "Let my form be thus, let my form not be thus." But because form is non-self, it leads to affliction, and one cannot have it so. The same is said of feeling, perception, formations and consciousness.
2. **Impermanence and suffering.** Each aggregate is impermanent; what is impermanent is suffering; and what is impermanent, suffering and subject to change is not fit to be regarded as "this is mine, this I am, this is my self" ({{term|etaṃ mama, esohamasmi, eso me attā}}).

Every kind of form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness, whether past, future or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near, is to be seen with right wisdom as it really is: "This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self."

## Personality view

The view that holds the aggregates to be a self is called personality view ({{term|sakkāyadiṭṭhi}}). The suttas describe it in twenty forms: the uninstructed worldling regards each of the five aggregates as self, or self as possessing it, or it as in self, or self as in it.[^mn44] Personality view is the first of the ten fetters, and it is abandoned at stream-entry.[^an1013]

## In the commentaries

The *Visuddhimagga* explains that the aggregates are non-self because there is no exercising of power over them ({{term|avasavattanato}}): the mode of being insusceptible to the exercise of power is the characteristic of non-self.[^vism21] They are non-self in the sense of having no core ({{term|asārakaṭṭhena}}), because there is no core of a self conceived as an abider, a doer, an experiencer or one who is master of itself.[^vism20] They are non-self for four further reasons: in the sense of voidness, of having no owner, of having no overlord, and of opposing self.[^vism21]

The characteristic of non-self is concealed by compactness ({{term|ghana}}). When what appears to be a single solid whole is resolved by analysis into its elements, the characteristic of non-self becomes apparent in its true nature.[^vism21]

## Contemplating non-self

By the contemplation of non-self ({{term|anattānupassanā}}), the meditator abandons the perception of self ({{term|attasaññā}}).[^vism21] With the full penetration of non-self at the noble path, personality view is cut off, and at arahantship the last trace of the conceit "I am" is removed.
