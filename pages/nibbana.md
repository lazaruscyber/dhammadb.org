**Nibbāna** ({{lang|Pāli|nibbāna}}) is the cessation of suffering, the third of the [[Four Noble Truths]] and the final goal of the Blessed One's teaching. In the suttas it is described as the destruction of lust, hatred and delusion,[^sn381] and as the unborn, unbecome, unmade and unconditioned, because of which an escape from the born, become, made and conditioned can be known.[^ud83]

Of the four truths, the cessation of suffering is the one to be realised ({{term|sacchikātabba}}).[^sn5611] The way to its realisation is the [[Noble Eightfold Path]].[^sn381] In the Abhidhamma, Nibbāna is the only unconditioned reality, and the object of the noble paths and fruits.[^abhs6]

## Meaning of the word

The commentarial tradition explains the word as *ni* + *vāna*: *ni* expresses departure or absence, and *vāna* is a name for craving, which binds and weaves together one existence with the next. Nibbāna is so called because it is a departure from craving.[^abhs6][^abhvbh]

The suttas also use the image of a flame going out, which is the ordinary meaning of the verb *nibbāyati*. The Ratana Sutta says of those whose old kamma is exhausted and who produce no new kamma:

:::quote Ratana Sutta (Khuddakapāṭha 6; Sutta Nipāta 2.1)
*Te khīṇabījā avirūḷhichandā, nibbanti dhīrā yathāyaṃ padīpo.*

Their seeds destroyed, their desires no longer growing, the wise go out like this lamp.
:::

## Nibbāna in the suttas

### The destruction of lust, hatred and delusion

When the wanderer Jambukhādaka asked the Venerable Sāriputta what Nibbāna is, the elder answered: "The destruction of lust, the destruction of hatred, the destruction of delusion: this, friend, is called Nibbāna." Asked whether there is a path for realising it, he answered that the path is the Noble Eightfold Path.[^sn381]

The commentary adds that Nibbāna is not the mere destruction of the defilements. It is called the destruction of lust and the rest because, in dependence upon it, lust and the rest are destroyed.[^spk]

### The cessation of craving

In the first discourse the Blessed One defined the noble truth of the cessation of suffering as the complete fading away and cessation of craving: its giving up, relinquishing, release and non-attachment.[^sn5611]

### The unconditioned

:::quote Udāna 8.3
*Atthi, bhikkhave, ajātaṃ abhūtaṃ akataṃ asaṅkhataṃ.*

There is, bhikkhus, an unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned. If there were not this unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned, no escape would be known here from the born, become, made and conditioned. But since there is an unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned, an escape is known from the born, become, made and conditioned.
:::

In the same collection the Blessed One spoke of that base ({{term|āyatana}}) where there is neither earth, water, fire nor air, neither this world nor another world, neither sun nor moon, neither coming nor going, neither arising nor passing away: just this is the end of suffering.[^ud81]

### The two elements of Nibbāna

The Itivuttaka distinguishes two elements of Nibbāna ({{term|nibbānadhātu}}):[^iti44]

| Element | Pāli | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| With residue remaining | {{term|sa-upādisesa-nibbānadhātu}} | Attained by an arahant during life. The five faculties remain, and the arahant still experiences the agreeable and disagreeable, but lust, hatred and delusion have been destroyed |
| Without residue remaining | {{term|anupādisesa-nibbānadhātu}} | Attained at the arahant's passing away, when all that is felt, not being delighted in, becomes cool here and now |

The commentaries explain "residue" as the aggregates that result from past clinging, which remain until the arahant's life ends.[^vism16c]

### The highest happiness

:::quote Dhammapada, verses 203 and 204
*Nibbānaṃ paramaṃ sukhaṃ.*

Nibbāna is the highest happiness.
:::

When a bhikkhu asked the Venerable Sāriputta how there could be happiness where nothing is felt, the elder answered that just this is the happiness: that nothing is felt there.[^an934]

### Beyond reckoning

When the wanderer Vacchagotta asked where the Tathāgata goes after death, the Blessed One answered with the simile of a fire. When a fire that burned in dependence on grass and sticks goes out, one cannot say that it has gone to the east, west, north or south; it is simply reckoned as "gone out". In the same way, the Tathāgata, released from reckoning in terms of the aggregates, is deep, immeasurable and hard to fathom, like the great ocean.[^mn72]

## In the Abhidhamma and commentaries

### An ultimate reality

The Abhidhamma recognises four ultimate realities ({{term|paramattha dhamma}}): consciousness ({{term|citta}}), mental factors ({{term|cetasika}}), matter ({{term|rūpa}}) and Nibbāna. The first three are conditioned; Nibbāna alone is unconditioned.[^abhs1] It is supramundane ({{term|lokuttara}}), is realised by the knowledge of the four paths, and is the object of path and fruition consciousness.[^abhs6]

### Characteristic, function and manifestation

The *Visuddhimagga* describes Nibbāna by the commentarial method of characteristic, function and manifestation:[^vism16d]

| Aspect | Description |
|---|---|
| Characteristic ({{term|lakkhaṇa}}) | Peace ({{term|santi}}) |
| Function ({{term|rasa}}) | Not dying ({{term|accuta}}), or giving comfort ({{term|assāsakaraṇa}}) |
| Manifestation ({{term|paccupaṭṭhāna}}) | The signless ({{term|animitta}}), or non-proliferation ({{term|nippapañca}}) |

### One, two and three

According to the *Abhidhammattha Saṅgaha*, Nibbāna is single in its intrinsic nature, as peace. It is twofold as the element with residue remaining and without residue remaining. It is threefold according to the aspects under which it is realised: as the empty ({{term|suññata}}), the signless ({{term|animitta}}) and the desireless ({{term|appaṇihita}}).[^abhs6]

### Nibbāna is not non-existence

The *Visuddhimagga* answers the view that Nibbāna is non-existent because ordinary people cannot apprehend it. It is apprehendable by the right means, the noble path, and what the foolish ordinary person does not apprehend is not therefore unapprehendable. And if Nibbāna did not exist, the right practice, made up of virtue, concentration and wisdom, would be futile; but it is not futile, since it reaches Nibbāna.[^vism16e]

## Realising Nibbāna

Nibbāna is realised in four stages, at each of which a path ({{term|magga}}) and its fruit ({{term|phala}}) take Nibbāna as their object and cut off certain fetters ({{term|saṃyojana}}):[^an1013][^mn6]

| Stage | Pāli | Fetters abandoned |
|---|---|---|
| Stream-entry | {{term|sotāpatti}} | Personality view, doubt, and clinging to rules and observances |
| Once-returning | {{term|sakadāgāmi}} | No further fetters cut, but lust, hatred and delusion are weakened |
| Non-returning | {{term|anāgāmi}} | Sensual desire and ill will |
| Arahantship | {{term|arahatta}} | Desire for form existence, desire for formless existence, conceit, restlessness and ignorance |

With arahantship all ten fetters have been abandoned, the holy life has been lived, and there is no more coming back to any state of existence.
