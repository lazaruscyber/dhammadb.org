The **Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta** ({{lang|Pāli|Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta}}), "The Discourse on Setting in Motion the Wheel of the Dhamma", is the first discourse given by the Buddha after the awakening. The Blessed One taught it to the group of five monks in the Deer Park at Isipatana, near Bārāṇasī.[^sn] In it the Tathāgata rejects the two extremes of sensual indulgence and self-torment, makes known the Middle Way, which is the [[Noble Eightfold Path]], and sets out the [[Four Noble Truths]] in their three phases and twelve aspects. At the end of the discourse the Venerable Koṇḍañña gained the vision of the Dhamma and became the first of the Buddha's disciples to attain the path.[^sn]

The discourse is preserved in two places in the [[Tipiṭaka]]: in the Sutta Piṭaka as the eleventh discourse of the Saccasaṃyutta of the [[Saṃyutta Nikāya]], and in the Vinaya Piṭaka, where the Mahāvagga includes it in its account of the first events after the awakening.[^mv]

## Background

{{see also|Ariyapariyesana Sutta}}

After the awakening beneath the Bodhi tree, the Blessed One considered to whom the Dhamma should first be taught. The Blessed One thought first of Āḷāra Kālāma, the Bodhisatta's former teacher, judging him wise and able to understand it quickly, but a deity informed the Blessed One that Āḷāra Kālāma had died seven days before.[^mn26] The Blessed One then thought of Uddaka Rāmaputta, and learned that he too had died. The Teacher next recalled the group of five monks who had attended the Bodhisatta during the years of striving, and saw that they were living in the Deer Park at Isipatana.[^mn26]

On the road between Gayā and the place of awakening the Blessed One met the ascetic Upaka, who asked who the Blessed One's teacher was. The Buddha declared that the Fully Enlightened One had no teacher and had awakened through direct knowledge alone. Upaka answered, "May it be so, friend", shook his head and went off by a side road.[^mn26]

When the five monks saw the Blessed One approaching, they agreed among themselves not to rise in greeting, since they believed the ascetic Gotama had given up the striving and turned to luxury. As the Blessed One came near they could not keep their agreement: one took the Blessed One's bowl and robe, one prepared a seat, and one brought water for the Blessed One's feet. They still addressed the Blessed One by name and as "friend" ({{term|āvuso}}). The Buddha told them not to address the Tathāgata in that way, declared that the Deathless had been attained, and asked whether the Tathāgata had ever spoken to them like this before. When they acknowledged that this had never happened, they became willing to listen.[^mn26]

According to the commentaries, the Buddha began the discourse on the full-moon day of the month of Āsāḷha, as the sun was setting in the west and the full moon was rising in the east. Besides the five monks, a great assembly of devas and eighteen crores (180 million) of Brahmās had gathered to hear it.[^jn][^sa]

## The discourse

### The two extremes

The Buddha opened by saying that there are two extremes that one who has gone forth should not follow:

| Extreme | Pāli | The Buddha's description |
|---|---|---|
| Devotion to the pleasure of sensual things | {{term|kāmesu kāmasukhallikānuyoga}} | Low ({{term|hīna}}), vulgar ({{term|gamma}}), the way of ordinary people ({{term|pothujjanika}}), ignoble ({{term|anariya}}) and unbeneficial ({{term|anatthasaṃhita}}) |
| Devotion to self-torment | {{term|attakilamathānuyoga}} | Painful ({{term|dukkha}}), ignoble and unbeneficial |

The commentary explains that "sensual things" here has two senses: the objects of sensual desire ({{term|vatthukāma}}) and sensual desire itself as a defilement ({{term|kilesakāma}}).[^sa]

### The Middle Way

Avoiding both extremes, the Tathāgata awakened to the Middle Way ({{term|majjhimā paṭipadā}}). It gives vision and knowledge, and leads to peace, to direct knowledge, to full awakening and to Nibbāna ({{term|cakkhukaraṇī ñāṇakaraṇī upasamāya abhiññāya sambodhāya nibbānāya saṃvattati}}). The Buddha identified this Middle Way as the [[Noble Eightfold Path]]: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration.[^sn]

### The Four Noble Truths

{{main|Four Noble Truths}}

The Buddha then set out the Four Noble Truths:[^sn]

1. **The noble truth of suffering** ({{term|dukkha ariyasacca}}). Birth, ageing, sickness and death are suffering; so are union with what is disliked, separation from what is liked, and not getting what one wants. In brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are suffering.
2. **The noble truth of the origin of suffering** ({{term|dukkhasamudaya ariyasacca}}). It is craving, which leads to renewed existence, is bound up with delight and lust, and seeks delight now here, now there: craving for sensual pleasures ({{term|kāmataṇhā}}), craving for existence ({{term|bhavataṇhā}}) and craving for non-existence ({{term|vibhavataṇhā}}).
3. **The noble truth of the cessation of suffering** ({{term|dukkhanirodha ariyasacca}}). It is the complete fading away and cessation of that same craving: its giving up, relinquishing, release and non-attachment.
4. **The noble truth of the way leading to the cessation of suffering** ({{term|dukkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā ariyasacca}}). It is the Noble Eightfold Path.

### Three phases and twelve aspects

The Buddha declared that with regard to each truth, vision, knowledge, wisdom, true knowledge and light had arisen in the Tathāgata concerning things not heard before. The Tathāgata knew each truth in three phases ({{term|tiparivaṭṭa}}): knowing the truth itself, knowing the task it calls for, and knowing that the task had been done. Four truths in three phases make twelve aspects ({{term|dvādasākāra}}).[^sn] The commentaries name the three phases knowledge of the truth ({{term|sacca-ñāṇa}}), knowledge of the task ({{term|kicca-ñāṇa}}) and knowledge of what has been done ({{term|kata-ñāṇa}}).[^sa]

| Truth | Knowledge of the truth | Knowledge of the task | Knowledge of what has been done |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suffering | This is suffering | It is to be fully understood ({{term|pariññeyya}}) | It has been fully understood |
| Origin | This is the origin of suffering | It is to be abandoned ({{term|pahātabba}}) | It has been abandoned |
| Cessation | This is the cessation of suffering | It is to be realised ({{term|sacchikātabba}}) | It has been realised |
| Path | This is the way to the cessation | It is to be developed ({{term|bhāvetabba}}) | It has been developed |

### The declaration of awakening

The Buddha stated that as long as this knowledge and vision of the Four Noble Truths in their three phases and twelve aspects was not fully purified, the Tathāgata did not claim to have awakened to unsurpassed perfect awakening in the world with its devas, Māras and Brahmās, its ascetics and brahmins, its rulers and people. Only when it was fully purified did the Blessed One make that claim.[^sn] The knowledge and vision then arose in the Tathāgata:

:::quote SN 56.11
*Akuppā me vimutti, ayam antimā jāti, natthi dāni punabbhavo.*

Unshakeable is my liberation. This is my last birth. Now there is no more renewed existence.
:::

## Koṇḍañña's attainment

While the discourse was being spoken, the dustless, stainless vision of the Dhamma ({{term|dhammacakkhu}}) arose in the Venerable Koṇḍañña:[^sn]

:::quote SN 56.11
*Yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ, sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhammaṃ.*

Whatever is subject to arising is all subject to cessation.
:::

With this he became a stream-enterer ({{term|sotāpanna}}), the first person to penetrate the Dhamma taught by the Buddha. The Buddha then uttered, "Koṇḍañña has indeed understood! Koṇḍañña has indeed understood!" ({{term|aññāsi vata bho Koṇḍañño}}). From this, the Venerable Koṇḍañña came to be known as Aññā Koṇḍañña, "Koṇḍañña who has understood".[^sn]

The commentaries add that eighteen crores of Brahmās were established in the fruit of stream-entry together with Koṇḍañña.[^sa][^jn]

## The proclamation of the devas

When the Buddha had set the Wheel of the Dhamma in motion, the earth-dwelling devas raised the cry that at Bārāṇasī, in the Deer Park at Isipatana, the Blessed One had set in motion the unsurpassed Wheel of the Dhamma, which cannot be stopped by any ascetic or brahmin, deva, Māra or Brahmā, or by anyone in the world.[^sn]

The cry was taken up in turn by the devas of each heaven:

:::columns 2
- The Four Great Kings ({{term|Cātumahārājikā}})
- The Thirty-three ({{term|Tāvatiṃsa}})
- The Yāma devas
- The Contented devas ({{term|Tusitā}})
- The devas who delight in creating ({{term|Nimmānaratī}})
- The devas who control the creations of others ({{term|Paranimmitavasavattī}})
- The devas of Brahmā's company ({{term|Brahmakāyikā}})
:::

At that moment the sound reached the Brahma world, the ten-thousandfold world-system shook and trembled, and a boundless, sublime light appeared in the world, surpassing the divine majesty of the devas.[^sn]

## After the discourse

Having seen and penetrated the Dhamma and gone beyond doubt, the Venerable Aññā Koṇḍañña asked to receive the going forth and the higher ordination under the Blessed One. The Buddha said, "Come, monk ({{term|ehi bhikkhu}}), the Dhamma is well proclaimed; live the holy life for the complete ending of suffering." That was his ordination, making him the first monk of the Buddha's dispensation.[^mv]

The Buddha then continued to instruct the remaining four with talks on the Dhamma. While the Blessed One taught, three of the monks went for alms, and the six lived on what they brought back. In this way the vision of the Dhamma arose first in the Venerables Vappa and Bhaddiya, and then in the Venerables Mahānāma and Assaji, and each was ordained in the same way.[^mv] The commentaries relate that the four attained stream-entry one after another on the days following the first discourse, beginning with Vappa on the day after the full moon.[^aa]

On the fifth day after the full moon the Buddha taught the five monks the [[Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta]], the Discourse on the Characteristic of Non-self. At its end all five attained arahantship, and there were then six arahants in the world.[^mv][^aa]

## Commentarial explanations

### The name

The title means "the discourse that sets in motion the Wheel of the Dhamma". According to the commentaries, the Wheel of the Dhamma ({{term|dhammacakka}}) stands for two kinds of the Buddha's knowledge:[^sa]

- **Knowledge of penetration** ({{term|paṭivedha-ñāṇa}}): the knowledge of the four paths, by which the Fully Enlightened One penetrated the Four Noble Truths at the foot of the Bodhi tree.
- **Knowledge of teaching** ({{term|desanā-ñāṇa}}): the knowledge by which the Tathāgata made the truths known to others, so that they too could penetrate them.

The Wheel is said to have been set in motion when, through this teaching, the vision of the Dhamma first arose in another being: the Venerable Koṇḍañña.[^sa]

### The truths seen together

Although the discourse describes the truths one by one, the commentarial tradition explains that at the moment of path knowledge all four are penetrated together, in a single moment: suffering is penetrated by full understanding, its origin by abandoning, cessation by realisation with Nibbāna as object, and the path by development.[^vism]

## The first discourse in Theravāda practice

The full-moon day of Āsāḷha is remembered in Theravāda countries as the day the Wheel of the Dhamma was set in motion and the Triple Gem of Buddha, Dhamma and Saṅgha became complete, with the first ordained disciple. The discourse is widely recited and chanted, and is often learned by heart in Pāli.
