The **Four Noble Truths** ({{lang|Pāli|cattāri ariyasaccāni}}) are the core of the Buddha's teaching. He set them out in his first discourse, given to the group of five ascetics at the Deer Park at Isipatana, near Bārāṇasī.[^sn5611] The truths describe suffering ({{term|dukkha}}), its origin, its cessation, and the way leading to its cessation.

## The four truths

{{main|Dukkha}}

| Truth | Pāli | Task |
|---|---|---|
| Suffering | {{term|dukkha ariyasacca}} | To be fully understood ({{term|pariññeyya}}) |
| Origin of suffering | {{term|dukkhasamudaya ariyasacca}} | To be abandoned ({{term|pahātabba}}) |
| Cessation of suffering | {{term|dukkhanirodha ariyasacca}} | To be realised ({{term|sacchikātabba}}) |
| Path to the cessation | {{term|dukkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā ariyasacca}} | To be developed ({{term|bhāvetabba}}) |

### Suffering

Birth, ageing, sickness and death are suffering; so are union with what is disliked, separation from what is liked, and not getting what one wants. In brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are suffering.[^sn5611]

### Origin

The origin of suffering is craving ({{term|taṇhā}}), which leads to renewed existence. It is threefold: craving for sensual pleasures ({{term|kāmataṇhā}}), craving for existence ({{term|bhavataṇhā}}) and craving for non-existence ({{term|vibhavataṇhā}}).

### Cessation

The cessation of suffering is the complete fading away and ending of that same craving: its giving up, release and non-attachment. This is [[Nibbāna]].

### The path

The way leading to the cessation of suffering is the [[Noble Eightfold Path]].

## Three turnings and twelve aspects

In the first discourse the Buddha explains that he knew each truth in three ways: knowing the truth itself, knowing the task it calls for, and knowing that the task had been done. Four truths in three turnings give twelve aspects. He declared that only when this knowledge was fully purified did he claim to have awakened.[^sn5611]

:::quote From the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (SN 56.11)
As long as my knowledge and vision of these Four Noble Truths as they really are, in their three phases and twelve aspects, was not thoroughly purified, I did not claim to have awakened.
:::

At the end of the discourse the ascetic Koṇḍañña gained the stainless vision of the Dhamma, becoming the first disciple to see the truth.{{ref|SN 56.11; see also the account in Vinaya, Mahāvagga 1.}}

:::note Study tip
Each truth is paired with a task. Reading them as tasks, rather than as statements to believe, is how the commentarial tradition presents them.
:::

[^sn5611]: Saṃyutta Nikāya 56.11, *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*.
