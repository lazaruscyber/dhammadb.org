**Samādhi** ({{lang|Pāli|samādhi}}) is concentration: the wholesome unification of mind on a single object. It is the second of the three trainings, resting on [[Sīla|virtue]] and supporting [[Paññā|wisdom]]. The Blessed One said: "Develop concentration, monks. One who is concentrated understands things as they really are."[^sn561]

## In the Noble Eightfold Path

Three factors of the [[Noble Eightfold Path]] make up the training in concentration:[^mn44]

| Factor | Pāli | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Right effort | {{term|sammā-vāyāma}} | The four right efforts: to prevent and abandon unwholesome states, and to arouse and maintain wholesome ones |
| Right mindfulness | {{term|sammā-sati}} | The four foundations of mindfulness: contemplating body, feelings, mind and phenomena |
| Right concentration | {{term|sammā-samādhi}} | The four jhānas |

## Meaning of the word

The *Visuddhimagga* defines concentration as profitable unification of mind ({{term|kusala-cittekaggatā}}). It is called *samādhi* in the sense of concentrating ({{term|samādhāna}}): placing consciousness and its concomitants evenly and rightly on a single object, so that they remain there undistracted and unscattered.[^vism3]

| Aspect | Description |
|---|---|
| Characteristic | Non-distraction |
| Function | Eliminating distraction |
| Manifestation | Non-wavering |
| Proximate cause | Happiness ({{term|sukha}}) |

## The four jhānas

Right concentration is defined in the suttas as the four meditative absorptions ({{term|jhāna}}).[^sn458] The commentaries describe each jhāna by the mental factors present in it:[^vism4]

| Jhāna | Factors |
|---|---|
| First | Applied thought ({{term|vitakka}}), sustained thought ({{term|vicāra}}), rapture ({{term|pīti}}), happiness ({{term|sukha}}), one-pointedness ({{term|ekaggatā}}) |
| Second | Rapture, happiness, one-pointedness, with inner confidence |
| Third | Happiness and one-pointedness, with equanimity, mindfulness and clear comprehension |
| Fourth | Equanimity ({{term|upekkhā}}) and one-pointedness, with purity of mindfulness |

To enter the first jhāna the meditator must first be secluded from sensual pleasures and from unwholesome states, that is, from the five hindrances: sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and remorse, and doubt.[^dn2]

## Access and absorption

The commentaries distinguish two levels of concentration in development:[^vism4]

- **Access concentration** ({{term|upacāra-samādhi}}), which arises when the hindrances are suppressed, just before absorption.
- **Absorption concentration** ({{term|appanā-samādhi}}), which is the jhāna itself.

## Meditation subjects

The *Visuddhimagga* lists forty meditation subjects ({{term|kammaṭṭhāna}}) for developing concentration:[^vism3]

| Group | Number |
|---|---|
| Kasiṇas (devices such as earth, water and colours) | 10 |
| Foulnesses (stages of a corpse) | 10 |
| Recollections, including the recollection of the Buddha and mindfulness of breathing | 10 |
| Divine abidings: loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity | 4 |
| Immaterial states | 4 |
| Perception of the repulsiveness of food | 1 |
| Defining of the four elements | 1 |

## Serenity and insight

The Blessed One spoke of two qualities that partake of true knowledge: serenity ({{term|samatha}}) and insight ({{term|vipassanā}}). Serenity develops the mind and leads to the abandoning of lust; insight develops wisdom and leads to the abandoning of ignorance.[^an232] Concentration is thus the ground on which wisdom grows: concentration developed on the basis of virtue is of great fruit, and wisdom developed on the basis of concentration is of great fruit.[^dn16]
