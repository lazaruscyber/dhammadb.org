**Sīla** ({{lang|Pāli|sīla}}) is virtue, or moral conduct: the restraint of body and speech from wrongdoing. It is the first of the three trainings, together with [[Samādhi|concentration]] and [[Paññā|wisdom]], and the foundation on which the other two rest. The Blessed One taught that concentration developed on the basis of virtue is of great fruit, and wisdom developed on the basis of concentration is of great fruit.[^dn16]

:::quote Saṃyutta Nikāya 1.23
*Sīle patiṭṭhāya naro sapañño, cittaṃ paññañca bhāvayaṃ.*

A wise person, established in virtue, developing the mind and wisdom...
:::

The *Visuddhimagga*, the great Theravāda manual of the path, opens with this verse and devotes its first two chapters to virtue.[^vism1]

## In the Noble Eightfold Path

Three factors of the [[Noble Eightfold Path]] make up the training in virtue:[^mn44]

| Factor | Pāli | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Right speech | {{term|sammā-vācā}} | Abstaining from false speech, divisive speech, harsh speech and idle chatter |
| Right action | {{term|sammā-kammanta}} | Abstaining from killing, stealing and sexual misconduct |
| Right livelihood | {{term|sammā-ājīva}} | Giving up wrong livelihood and earning a living by right livelihood |

## Meaning of the word

The *Visuddhimagga* explains that virtue is called *sīla* in the sense of composing ({{term|sīlana}}): it keeps bodily and verbal action from being scattered, and it upholds profitable states as their foundation. It describes virtue by its characteristic, function, manifestation and proximate cause:[^vism1]

| Aspect | Description |
|---|---|
| Characteristic | Composing ({{term|sīlana}}) |
| Function | Stopping misconduct, and the quality of blamelessness |
| Manifestation | Purity of body, speech and mind |
| Proximate cause | Conscience and shame ({{term|hiri-ottappa}}) |

## The precepts

Virtue is undertaken in sets of training rules ({{term|sikkhāpada}}) suited to different ways of life.

**The five precepts** ({{term|pañcasīla}}) are the virtue of the lay follower: abstaining from killing living beings, from taking what is not given, from sexual misconduct, from false speech, and from intoxicating drinks and drugs that cause heedlessness.

**The eight precepts** ({{term|aṭṭhaṅga-sīla}}) are kept on [[Uposatha]] days. The third precept becomes complete abstinence from sexual activity, and three are added: not eating after midday, not watching entertainments or wearing adornments, and not using high and luxurious beds.[^an370]

**The ten precepts** are kept by novices ({{term|sāmaṇera}}), who also refrain from accepting gold and silver.

**The Pātimokkha** is the code of training rules for fully ordained monks and nuns, laid down in the Vinaya Piṭaka and recited on the Uposatha days.

## The fourfold purity of virtue

The commentaries describe the virtue of a monk as fourfold purity ({{term|catupārisuddhi-sīla}}):[^vism1]

1. **Restraint according to the Pātimokkha** ({{term|pātimokkha-saṃvara}}): keeping the monastic training rules.
2. **Restraint of the sense faculties** ({{term|indriya-saṃvara}}): guarding the eye, ear, nose, tongue, body and mind so that covetousness and grief do not invade them.
3. **Purification of livelihood** ({{term|ājīva-pārisuddhi}}): obtaining the requisites without wrong means such as scheming or hinting.
4. **Virtue concerning the requisites** ({{term|paccaya-sannissita}}): using robes, almsfood, lodging and medicine only after reflecting on their purpose.

## The benefits of virtue

The Blessed One named five benefits for one who is virtuous:[^dn16b]

1. Great wealth through heedfulness.
2. A good reputation.
3. Confidence and composure in any assembly.
4. Dying unconfused.
5. Rebirth in a good destination, a heavenly world, after death.

Beyond these, virtue leads to freedom from remorse, and so to gladness, joy, tranquillity, happiness and concentration, and on to knowledge and liberation.[^an101]
