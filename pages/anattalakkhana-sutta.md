The **Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta** ({{lang|Pāli|Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta}}), "The Discourse on the Characteristic of Non-self", is the second discourse given by the Buddha. The Blessed One taught it to the group of five monks in the Deer Park at Isipatana, near Bārāṇasī, after all five had become stream-enterers. At its end the minds of all five were liberated from the taints, and there were then six arahants in the world.[^mv][^sn2259]

## Background

{{main|Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta}}

At the end of the first discourse the Venerable Koṇḍañña gained the vision of the Dhamma. In the following days, while the Blessed One continued to instruct the others, the vision of the Dhamma arose in the Venerables Vappa, Bhaddiya, Mahānāma and Assaji.[^mv] According to the commentaries, the Blessed One taught the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta on the fifth day after the full moon of [[Āsāḷha]].[^aa]

## The discourse

### Form is not self

The Blessed One began with form: "Form, monks, is not self. If form were self, this form would not lead to affliction, and it would be possible to have it of form: 'Let my form be thus; let my form not be thus.' But because form is not self, it leads to affliction, and it is not possible to have it of form: 'Let my form be thus; let my form not be thus.'"[^sn2259] The same was then said of feeling, perception, formations and consciousness.

### The question and answer

The Blessed One then questioned the monks about each of the five aggregates:[^sn2259]

| Question | Answer of the five monks |
|---|---|
| Is form permanent or impermanent? | Impermanent, venerable sir. |
| Is what is impermanent suffering or happiness? | Suffering, venerable sir. |
| Is what is impermanent, suffering and subject to change fit to be regarded thus: "This is mine, this I am, this is my self"? | No, venerable sir. |

The same questions were asked and answered for feeling, perception, formations and consciousness.

### Seeing with right wisdom

Therefore, the Blessed One concluded, every kind of form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness, whether past, future or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, inferior or superior, far or near, should be seen as it really is with right wisdom: "This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self" ({{term|netaṃ mama, nesohamasmi, na meso attā}}).[^sn2259]

### Liberation

Seeing in this way, the instructed noble disciple becomes disenchanted ({{term|nibbindati}}) with the aggregates. Through disenchantment, lust fades away ({{term|virajjati}}). Through the fading of lust, the mind is liberated. When it is liberated, there comes the knowledge: "It is liberated." The disciple understands: "Destroyed is birth, the holy life has been lived, what was to be done has been done, there is no more coming back to any state of being."[^sn2259]

## The result

The five monks delighted in the Blessed One's words, and while the discourse was being spoken their minds were liberated from the taints through non-clinging. The Vinaya records that at that time there were six arahants in the world.[^mv]

## Significance

The discourse sets out the characteristic of [[Anattā|non-self]], and through it the three characteristics together: what is impermanent is suffering, and what is impermanent and suffering is not fit to be taken as self. Together with the [[Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta]] it is among the most widely recited discourses in the Theravāda world.
