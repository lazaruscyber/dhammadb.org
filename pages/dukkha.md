**Dukkha** ({{lang|Pāli|dukkha}}) is the first of the [[Four Noble Truths]] and one of the three characteristics of all conditioned things. The word is usually rendered as "suffering", but in the teaching it covers far more than pain: it includes the unsatisfactoriness of everything that arises and passes away. The Blessed One summed up the whole of the teaching in terms of it: suffering and the cessation of suffering.[^sn2286]

Of the four truths, suffering is the one to be fully understood ({{term|pariññeyya}}).[^sn5611] Its origin is craving, its cessation is [[Nibbāna]], and the way to its cessation is the [[Noble Eightfold Path]].

## Meaning of the word

### In the commentaries

The *Visuddhimagga* explains the word by its two parts. The syllable *du* is used in the sense of vile ({{term|kucchita}}), as in *duputta*, "a bad son"; the syllable *khaṃ* is used in the sense of empty ({{term|tuccha}}), as when empty space is called *khaṃ*. The first truth is called *dukkha* because it is vile, being the haunt of many dangers, and empty, being devoid of the permanence, beauty, pleasure and self that foolish people attribute to it.[^vism16]

Elsewhere it is explained as "that which makes beings suffer" ({{term|dukkhayati}}).[^vism17]

### Painful feeling

In a narrower sense *dukkha* is one of the three kinds of feeling: pleasant ({{term|sukha}}), painful ({{term|dukkha}}) and neither painful nor pleasant ({{term|adukkhamasukha}}).[^sn36] The Abhidhamma divides painful feeling further into bodily pain ({{term|dukkha}}) and mental displeasure ({{term|domanassa}}).[^dhs] Bodily pain can still be felt by an arahant, but mental displeasure has been abandoned.

## The noble truth of suffering

{{main|Four Noble Truths}}

In the first discourse the Blessed One defined the noble truth of suffering, and the definition is analysed in detail in the Saccavibhaṅga Sutta and the Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta:[^sn5611][^mn141]

| Suffering | Pāli | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Birth | {{term|jāti}} | The coming into being of beings, the appearance of the aggregates and the acquiring of the sense bases |
| Ageing | {{term|jarā}} | Decrepitude, broken teeth, grey hair, wrinkled skin, the decline of life and the weakening of the faculties |
| Death | {{term|maraṇa}} | Passing away, the breaking up of the aggregates and the laying down of the body |
| Sorrow | {{term|soka}} | The inner sorrow of one struck by misfortune |
| Lamentation | {{term|parideva}} | Wailing and crying out at misfortune |
| Pain | {{term|dukkha}} | Bodily pain and bodily discomfort |
| Grief | {{term|domanassa}} | Mental pain and mental discomfort |
| Despair | {{term|upāyāsa}} | Distress and despair arising from misfortune |
| Not getting what one wants | {{term|yampicchaṃ na labhati}} | Wishing not to be subject to birth, ageing and death, and not obtaining it by wishing |

In brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging ({{term|pañcupādānakkhandhā}}) are suffering: form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness.[^sn5611][^mn141]

## Kinds of suffering

### The three kinds

When the wanderer Jambukhādaka asked the Venerable Sāriputta what suffering is, the elder answered that there are three kinds.[^sn3814] The commentaries explain them as follows:[^vism16b]

| Kind | Pāli | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Suffering as pain | {{term|dukkha-dukkhatā}} | Painful bodily and mental feeling, which is suffering both by nature and by name |
| Suffering in change | {{term|vipariṇāma-dukkhatā}} | Pleasant feeling, which becomes a cause of suffering when it changes |
| Suffering of formations | {{term|saṅkhāra-dukkhatā}} | All conditioned things of the three planes of existence, because they are oppressed by rise and fall |

The path for the full understanding of these three kinds of suffering is the [[Noble Eightfold Path]].[^sn3814][^sn45165]

### The seven kinds in the commentaries

The *Visuddhimagga* adds four further kinds to the three, making seven:[^vism16b]

- **Concealed suffering** ({{term|paṭicchanna-dukkha}}): bodily and mental affliction such as earache, toothache and the fever of lust or of hatred, which is known only by asking, because it is not openly visible.
- **Exposed suffering** ({{term|appaṭicchanna-dukkha}}): affliction such as that caused by torture, which is plain to see.
- **Indirect suffering** ({{term|pariyāya-dukkha}}): birth and the other items of the first truth, apart from intrinsic pain, which are called suffering because they are the basis for suffering of one kind or another.
- **Direct suffering** ({{term|nippariyāya-dukkha}}): suffering as pain itself.

### The four meanings

The *Paṭisambhidāmagga* gives four meanings in which suffering is real, not unreal, and not otherwise: the meaning of oppressing ({{term|pīḷana}}), of being formed ({{term|saṅkhata}}), of burning ({{term|santāpa}}) and of changing ({{term|vipariṇāma}}).[^ps]

## A characteristic of all conditioned things

Suffering is one of the three characteristics ({{term|tilakkhaṇa}}) of conditioned things, together with impermanence ({{term|anicca}}) and non-self ({{term|anattā}}). The Blessed One taught that what is impermanent is suffering, and what is suffering is non-self.[^sn2215]

:::quote Dhammapada, verse 278
*Sabbe saṅkhārā dukkhā ti, yadā paññāya passati;*
*atha nibbindati dukkhe, esa maggo visuddhiyā.*

When one sees with wisdom that all formations are suffering, one becomes disenchanted with suffering. This is the path to purity.
:::

The characteristic applies to formations ({{term|saṅkhārā}}), that is, to everything conditioned. [[Nibbāna]], being unconditioned, is not suffering.

In the second discourse, the [[Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta]], the Blessed One asked the five monks whether each of the aggregates is permanent or impermanent, and whether what is impermanent is suffering or happiness. They answered that it is suffering, and so came to see that none of the aggregates is fit to be regarded as "mine", "I" or "my self".[^sn2259]

## Pleasure and suffering

The teaching on suffering does not deny that pleasure exists. To Mahāli the Licchavi the Blessed One explained that if form and the other aggregates were exclusively suffering, beings would not be enamoured of them. It is because they are also pleasurable that beings become attached to them and are defiled. And because they are also suffering, beings become disenchanted with them and are purified.[^sn2260]

The suttas analyse each aggregate in terms of its gratification ({{term|assāda}}), the pleasure that arises in dependence on it; its danger ({{term|ādīnava}}), that it is impermanent, suffering and subject to change; and the escape from it ({{term|nissaraṇa}}), the removal of desire and lust for it.[^sn2226]

## Suffering in the round of rebirth

The suffering of a single life is only a small part of the suffering of the round of rebirth ({{term|saṃsāra}}), whose beginning cannot be discovered. The Blessed One asked the monks which was greater: the tears they had shed while wandering on through this long course, weeping at union with the disliked and separation from the liked, or the water of the four great oceans. The tears, they answered, were greater, and the Blessed One confirmed it. Such a long experience of suffering, the Teacher said, is enough to become disenchanted with all formations and to be released from them.[^sn153]

## Understanding suffering

The task with regard to the first truth is full understanding ({{term|pariññā}}). In the first discourse the Tathāgata declared that it had been known that "this noble truth of suffering is to be fully understood" and that "it has been fully understood".[^sn5611] The commentaries explain that full understanding is reached when the noble path is attained: at that moment the truth of suffering is penetrated by full understanding, its origin by abandoning, its cessation by realisation and the path by development, all at once.[^vism22]

In the practice of insight the meditator contemplates formations as suffering ({{term|dukkhānupassanā}}), seeing them as continually oppressed by rise and fall. This contemplation overcomes the perception of pleasure in what is in fact suffering.[^vism21]
