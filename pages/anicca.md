**Anicca** ({{lang|Pāli|anicca}}), "impermanent", is the first of the three characteristics ({{term|tilakkhaṇa}}) of all conditioned things, together with [[Dukkha|suffering]] and [[Anattā|non-self]]. Everything that arises through conditions changes and passes away. The Blessed One taught that what is impermanent is suffering, and what is suffering is non-self,[^sn2215] so the understanding of impermanence opens the way to the understanding of the other two characteristics.

:::quote Dhammapada, verse 277
*Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā ti, yadā paññāya passati;*
*atha nibbindati dukkhe, esa maggo visuddhiyā.*

When one sees with wisdom that all formations are impermanent, one becomes disenchanted with suffering. This is the path to purity.
:::

## The meaning of impermanence

### In the suttas

The suttas name three characteristics by which the conditioned ({{term|saṅkhata}}) can be recognised: its arising is seen, its passing away is seen, and its alteration while it persists is seen.[^an347] Whatever is subject to arising is subject to cessation. This was the insight that arose in the Venerable Koṇḍañña at the end of the first discourse: *yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ, sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhammaṃ*, "whatever is subject to arising is all subject to cessation".[^sn5611]

In the [[Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta]] the Blessed One asked the five monks of each aggregate in turn: "Is form permanent or impermanent?" "Impermanent, venerable sir." "Is what is impermanent suffering or happiness?" "Suffering, venerable sir."[^sn2259]

### In the commentaries

The *Visuddhimagga* distinguishes the impermanent from the characteristic of impermanence. The five aggregates are impermanent because they rise and fall and change, or because they cease to exist after having been ({{term|hutvā abhāvato}}). Their rise, fall and change, or their mode of ceasing to exist after having been, is the characteristic of impermanence.[^vism21]

## What conceals impermanence

According to the *Visuddhimagga*, each of the three characteristics is hidden from one who does not give it attention:[^vism21]

| Characteristic | Concealed by | Revealed when |
|---|---|---|
| Impermanence | Continuity ({{term|santati}}) | Continuity is broken up by attending to rise and fall |
| Suffering | The postures ({{term|iriyāpatha}}) | The postures are exposed by attending to continual oppression |
| Non-self | Compactness ({{term|ghana}}) | Compactness is resolved by analysis into elements |

Because one moment of experience follows another without a break, things appear lasting. When the meditator attends to the rise and fall of each state, the states are seen to occur separately, one after another, and impermanence becomes plain.[^vism21]

## Contemplating impermanence

The contemplation of impermanence ({{term|aniccānupassanā}}) is the first of the insight contemplations. By contemplating formations as impermanent, the meditator abandons the perception of permanence ({{term|niccasaññā}}).[^ps][^vism21]

The suttas praise the perception of impermanence ({{term|aniccasaññā}}) highly. Developed and cultivated, it exhausts all sensual lust, all lust for form, all lust for existence and all ignorance, and uproots all conceit "I am".[^sn22102] The Blessed One said that developing the perception of impermanence even for the moment of a finger-snap is more fruitful than the greatest of gifts.[^an920]

## The last words of the Blessed One

At Kusinārā, just before the parinibbāna, the Blessed One gave a final exhortation to the monks about impermanence:

:::quote Mahāparinibbāna Sutta (DN 16)
*Vayadhammā saṅkhārā, appamādena sampādetha.*

Formations are subject to vanishing. Strive on with heedfulness.
:::
